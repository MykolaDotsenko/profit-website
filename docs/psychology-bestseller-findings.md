# Psychology Bestseller Findings — PROFIT Website

Research date: 2026-09-26

This document captures psychology and decision-science findings from major bestselling books plus stronger research evidence where popular-book claims require qualification.

The goal is not psychological manipulation. The goal is to improve:
- comprehension;
- trust;
- decision quality;
- perceived control;
- responsible uncertainty communication;
- qualified action.

## 1. Daniel Kahneman — Thinking, Fast and Slow

Useful model:

People often rely on fast, intuitive processing for first impressions and slower, effortful processing for difficult judgments.

Important caution:
Treat "System 1 / System 2" as a useful explanatory model, not as literal separate brain modules.

### PROFIT implication

The website needs two layers:

### Fast layer
Within seconds, a visitor should grasp:
- who PROFIT is for;
- the economic outcome;
- the core mechanism;
- the next action.

### Slow layer
For visitors who want to verify the claim, provide:
- methodology;
- data provenance;
- confidence;
- evidence state;
- limitations;
- examples;
- security/data-control details.

### Rule

**Simple first impression, inspectable depth.**

Do not force careful users to accept marketing claims without evidence.

## 2. Processing fluency

Psychology research shows that information that is easier to process can feel more familiar, plausible or true.

### PROFIT implication

Use fluency ethically:
- plain language;
- strong contrast;
- clear typography;
- predictable layouts;
- concrete examples;
- short labels;
- consistent metric formatting.

### Critical guardrail

**Clarity must make evidence easier to inspect, not weak claims easier to believe.**

Do not use fluent design to disguise uncertainty.

## 3. Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein — Nudge

Core idea:

Choice architecture affects decisions even when all options remain available.

### PROFIT implication

Structure should help visitors make an informed next step without reducing autonomy.

Good choice architecture:
- one clearly primary CTA;
- one meaningful secondary CTA;
- progressive disclosure;
- predictable navigation;
- clear form defaults;
- transparent explanations.

Bad choice architecture:
- preselected marketing consent;
- hidden decline options;
- fake urgency;
- confusing cancellation/contact paths;
- visually burying limitations.

### Rule

**Guide, do not trap.**

## 4. Choice overload — use with nuance

Popular psychology often says "more choices are always worse."

Research is more nuanced.

Meta-analytic work found that choice overload is highly context-dependent and is stronger when:
- the choice set is complex;
- the decision is difficult;
- preferences are uncertain;
- people are trying to minimize effort.

### PROFIT implication

Do not apply an arbitrary "3 options only" rule.

Instead reduce complexity where visitors are uncertain.

For the website:
- keep the primary navigation focused;
- distinguish farmer and investor journeys;
- avoid multiple competing CTAs in the same visual priority;
- group secondary content;
- progressively reveal technical depth.

### Rule

**Reduce decision complexity, not information access.**

## 5. Sendhil Mullainathan & Eldar Shafir — Scarcity

Core idea:

Scarcity of time, money or attention can narrow cognitive bandwidth and create tunneling.

### PROFIT implication

Farmers may visit the site while:
- busy;
- on mobile;
- between operations;
- under financial/time pressure;
- with limited attention.

Design for constrained attention:
- show essentials early;
- use concrete economic language;
- avoid long intros;
- minimize unnecessary steps;
- make forms short;
- support weak connectivity;
- let users save/return later where relevant.

### Rule

**Do not make a busy farmer work to understand the value proposition.**

### Ethical guardrail

Do not exploit scarcity with countdowns or artificial urgency.

## 6. Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein — Noise

Core idea:

Judgments can vary inconsistently even when the underlying case is similar.

### PROFIT implication

The website and future product should standardize how evidence is represented.

Examples:
- same confidence scale everywhere;
- same evidence-state vocabulary;
- same units;
- same economic-number formatting;
- same methodology structure;
- same thresholds where a formal standard exists.

Avoid:
- one page calling something "high confidence" while another uses "strong evidence" for the same state;
- arbitrary color changes;
- inconsistent precision.

### Rule

**Consistency reduces noise in user interpretation.**

## 7. Annie Duke — Thinking in Bets

Core idea:

A good decision can produce a bad outcome, and a bad decision can sometimes produce a good outcome because uncertainty and luck remain.

### PROFIT implication

This is extremely important for VEV and case studies.

Do not imply:
- good yield = good decision;
- bad yield = bad decision;
- one profitable season proves PROFIT caused the result.

Case studies should separate:
- information available at decision time;
- decision logic;
- expected outcome/range;
- actual outcome;
- external factors;
- attribution;
- confidence.

### Rule

**Judge decisions by process and evidence, not outcome alone.**

## 8. Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner — Superforecasting

Useful principles:
- gather evidence from multiple sources;
- think probabilistically;
- update estimates;
- keep score;
- admit error;
- revise when evidence changes.

### PROFIT implication

Avoid false precision.

Where forecasts are shown:
- use probability/range when appropriate;
- show confidence;
- show update date;
- distinguish forecast from observed outcome;
- allow model estimates to change with new evidence.

### Rule

**Calibrated uncertainty is stronger than fake certainty.**

## 9. Communicating uncertainty

Research reviews find that openly communicating epistemic uncertainty does not automatically destroy trust; effects depend on audience and format.

### PROFIT implication

Our VEV/evidence language can be a trust feature.

Examples:
- Modelled
- Observed
- Attributed
- Verified

Confidence:
- High
- Medium
- Low / insufficient evidence

For numbers:
- show ranges where appropriate;
- label assumptions;
- explain missing data;
- avoid excessive decimal precision.

### Rule

**Uncertainty should be visible, structured and understandable.**

## 10. Don Norman — The Design of Everyday Things

Core human-centered design ideas:
- signifiers;
- mapping;
- feedback;
- conceptual models;
- constraints;
- error prevention/recovery.

### PROFIT implication

Interactions should be obvious without instruction.

Examples:
- links look like navigation;
- buttons look actionable;
- selected states are visible;
- forms confirm success/failure;
- interactive metrics have clear affordances;
- no mystery icons without labels when meaning is not obvious.

### Rule

**Brand expression can be novel; interaction semantics should remain familiar.**

## 11. Habit psychology — limited relevance, but one useful principle

Books such as The Power of Habit and Atomic Habits emphasize the power of cues and reducing friction.

For a presentation website, this should not become behavioral manipulation.

Useful adaptation:
- make the primary next step obvious;
- reduce unnecessary form effort;
- provide immediate feedback after action;
- make return paths predictable.

### Rule

**Use behavioral design to remove friction, not create dependence.**

## 12. Loss aversion and framing — high risk of misuse

Prospect theory popularized the idea that losses can weigh more heavily than equivalent gains.

### PROFIT implication

Farm economics naturally contains losses, but do not weaponize fear.

Acceptable:
- "Where are costs increasing?"
- "Which fields are below target margin?"
- evidence-based avoided-cost scenarios.

Avoid:
- exaggerated loss headlines;
- unverified "you are losing €X every day";
- fear-based countdowns;
- cherry-picked downside framing.

### Rule

**Show economic downside only when it is evidence-based and decision-relevant.**

## 13. Trust through control

Across decision psychology and human-centered design, perceived control matters when people face uncertainty.

### PROFIT implication

The website should reinforce:
- farmer owns/controls their data;
- permissions are explicit;
- assumptions are inspectable;
- calculations are explainable;
- recommendations are not mandatory;
- the farmer can question/reject a recommendation.

### Rule

**The website should make the farmer feel more in control, not more dependent on an opaque system.**

## 14. Progressive disclosure is psychologically well matched to PROFIT

Visitors have different information needs.

### Fast visitor
Needs:
- one-line value proposition;
- product visual;
- CTA.

### Evaluating farmer
Needs:
- data requirements;
- examples;
- methodology;
- integrations;
- limitations.

### Investor/advisor
Needs:
- wedge;
- VEV;
- moat hypothesis;
- market/business model;
- evidence.

### Technical/research visitor
Needs:
- methodology;
- sources;
- definitions;
- security;
- provenance.

### Rule

**Keep the surface simple while preserving depth one layer away.**

## 15. Psychological safety in claim design

A credible site should reduce fear of being deceived.

Use:
- exact evidence labels;
- source annotations;
- dates;
- assumptions;
- confidence;
- clear distinction between actual and illustrative data.

Avoid:
- ambiguous "proven" language;
- uncited claims;
- overprecise projections;
- hidden assumptions.

## 16. Cognitive-load rule for sections

Each section should have:
- one primary question;
- one dominant visual idea;
- one dominant takeaway.

Secondary detail can exist, but should not compete equally.

### PROFIT homepage examples

Hero:
**What is PROFIT?**

Problem:
**Why is farm profitability hard to see?**

Mechanism:
**How does PROFIT turn data into economic meaning?**

Evidence:
**How strong is the claim?**

Trust:
**Can I rely on this company/system?**

CTA:
**What happens if I want to try it?**

## 17. Number psychology: avoid false precision

People can overinterpret precision.

### PROFIT rule

Do not display:
- €637.2847/ha

when the input uncertainty does not justify it.

Prefer:
- €637/ha
or
- €620–€660/ha

depending on evidence/model semantics.

Precision should communicate measurement quality, not visual sophistication.

## 18. Case-study psychology

A useful case study should counter hindsight and outcome bias.

Structure:

1. Context
2. Data available
3. Decision question
4. Alternatives
5. PROFIT analysis
6. Expected effect
7. Decision/action
8. Actual outcome
9. Attribution
10. Confidence
11. What was learned

This structure is stronger than:
"Farmer used PROFIT and made +12%."

## 19. Psychological anti-patterns to ban

Do not use:
- fake scarcity;
- countdown timers without real deadlines;
- confirmshaming;
- hidden opt-outs;
- pre-checked consent;
- false authority badges;
- fabricated testimonials;
- manipulated comparison tables;
- intentional information asymmetry;
- fear-heavy loss framing;
- confusing unsubscribe/contact controls.

## 20. Strongest combined psychology insight

**PROFIT should feel easy to understand but hard to fool yourself with.**

That means:

### Easy to understand
- fluent language;
- strong hierarchy;
- simple paths;
- concrete examples.

### Hard to fool yourself
- visible uncertainty;
- inspectable assumptions;
- decision vs outcome separation;
- confidence/evidence states;
- source provenance;
- no false precision.

This combination is especially aligned with Agricultural Decision Intelligence.

# Revised psychological journey

1. **Orientation** — "I understand what this is."
2. **Relevance** — "This matches a real problem I have."
3. **Control** — "I understand what data/action is required."
4. **Credibility** — "I can inspect the basis of the claim."
5. **Uncertainty calibration** — "I know what is known and unknown."
6. **Decision support** — "I can judge whether this is worth exploring."
7. **Action** — "I know the next step and it feels low-friction."

# Psychological acceptance criteria

Before launch:
- first-screen meaning is understandable in seconds;
- no section has multiple equally dominant calls to action;
- technical depth is available without cluttering the surface;
- evidence labels are consistent;
- uncertainty is communicated without jargon;
- assumptions are visible for quantitative examples;
- real/illustrative data cannot be confused;
- farmer control is explicit;
- navigation/action affordances are familiar;
- the site does not use dark patterns;
- case studies separate decision quality from outcome quality.
